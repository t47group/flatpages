Peak season brings out the incentives. Perfect-attendance bonuses for the week of Black Friday. A dollar more an hour for anyone who works every scheduled shift through December. A safety bonus for a crew that goes the month without an incident. These programs work. They also change how much overtime you owe, and that part is easy to miss.

Under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), overtime is not simply one and a half times the base hourly wage. It is one and a half times the **regular rate**, and most bonuses get folded into that number. Here is how it works and how to keep your peak incentives from turning into back-pay claims.

## What the regular rate includes

The Department of Labor's guidance starts with a simple rule: bonuses are part of the regular rate **unless a specific exclusion applies.** DOL Fact Sheet #56C lists common bonuses that must be included, because they are promised in advance and employees expect them:

- Attendance bonuses
- Production bonuses based on a formula, individual or group
- Quality and accuracy bonuses
- Bonuses announced to encourage faster or more efficient work
- Safety bonuses, such as for a number of days without an incident

The fact sheet adds a point employers sometimes argue with: the fact that you *could* decide not to pay a promised bonus does not make it discretionary.

## DOL confirmed this again in 2026

In January 2026, DOL's Wage and Hour Division issued Opinion Letter FLSA2026-2 on an incentive program that paid drivers extra per hour based on punctuality, attendance, safety tasks and efficiency. The employer had been calculating overtime on the base wage alone. DOL concluded the bonuses were nondiscretionary, because the terms were set in advance, and had to be included in the regular rate for each week they were earned.

## How the math works

Here is the example from DOL Fact Sheet #56C. An employee earns $10 an hour, works 43 hours and earns a $50 nondiscretionary bonus that week.

- Straight-time pay: $10 x 43 = $430
- Add the bonus: $430 + $50 = $480
- Regular rate: $480 / 43 = $11.16
- Half-time premium: $11.16 x 0.5 = $5.58
- Overtime premium for 3 hours: $16.74
- **Total due: $496.74**

If the employer had ignored the bonus and paid overtime on $10 alone, it would have paid $15 in overtime premium instead of $16.74. A small gap on one paycheck. Across a few hundred workers and six weeks of peak, it adds up, and back wages under the FLSA can come with liquidated damages.

The opinion letter shows the same effect at a larger scale. A worker earning $12 an hour plus $5.60 an hour in bonuses for a 50-hour week has a regular rate of $17.60, which means an $8.80 half-time premium on each of the 10 overtime hours, or $968 in total wages for the week.

## Bonuses that cover more than one week

Some incentives are paid at the end of a period, such as "work every scheduled shift from Thanksgiving through New Year's and receive $300." Federal regulations allow you to pay overtime on the base rate during the period and then, once the bonus is earned, **apportion it back over the weeks it covers** and pay extra overtime for the weeks that had overtime hours. If you can't tell how much of the bonus was earned in each week, the regulation allows a reasonable method, such as assuming it was earned evenly across the weeks or hours.

In practice, that means a true-up payment after the bonus period ends. Build it into your payroll calendar so it doesn't get forgotten in January.

## What can be excluded

Not every payment raises the regular rate. Under DOL guidance, these can generally be left out:

- **Discretionary bonuses,** but only if you keep sole discretion over whether to pay and how much until at or near the end of the period, and the bonus is not paid under a prior promise. A thank-you bonus decided after peak, with no advance announcement, can fit. A bonus you advertised in October does not.
- **Holiday gifts** that are not tied to hours worked, production or efficiency, such as the same gift card for everyone.
- **Holiday and weekend premiums** paid at one and a half times the normal rate or more, which can also be credited toward overtime owed.

The label you put on a payment does not decide the question. DOL looks at the facts.

## A connection to the new overtime deduction

There is one more reason to get the regular rate right this year. Starting with 2026 wages, employers must report "qualified overtime compensation" on Form W-2 for the federal no-tax-on-overtime deduction. That amount is the FLSA-required premium, calculated from the regular rate. If your regular rate is understated, the figure you report will be too. ## How to run peak incentives cleanly

1. **Write the rules down.** Spell out eligibility, the amount and the period. Clarity helps attendance and makes payroll easier.
2. **Tell payroll before the program starts,** so bonuses are coded as nondiscretionary and included in overtime calculations automatically.
3. **Plan the true-up** for any multi-week bonus.
4. **Use a premium of at least time and a half** if you want to pay extra for holiday shifts and credit it toward overtime.
5. **Coordinate with your staffing partner.** If you want temps included in an incentive, the agency pays it as employer of record and must include it in their regular rate. Agree in writing on the terms and how it will be billed, including the overtime effect.

## The bottom line

Peak incentives are a good tool for keeping shifts covered. Just remember that a promised bonus is part of pay, and pay above 40 hours carries a premium. Include nondiscretionary bonuses in the regular rate, true up multi-week bonuses and make sure your staffing partner does the same.
