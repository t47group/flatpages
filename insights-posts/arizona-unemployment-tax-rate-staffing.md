Most employers look at their unemployment insurance rate once a year, when the notice arrives, and then forget about it. But for operations with big seasonal swings, such as distribution centers that ramp up for peak or dealerships that staff up for year-end events, the way you hire and release people affects that rate for years.

Here is how Arizona's system works, what drives your rate up or down and what to do before the new rate year begins.

## The basics

Arizona employers pay state unemployment insurance (UI) tax to the Arizona Department of Economic Security (DES). A few key figures:

- **Taxable wage base:** UI tax applies to the first **$8,000** paid to each employee in a calendar year, under A.R.S. 23-622.
- **New employer rate:** New employers are assigned **2.0 percent** for at least two calendar years, according to DES.
- **2026 rates:** Based on the DES rate chart for 2026, rates ranged from 0.03 percent to 4.18 percent for employers with a positive reserve ratio and from 4.41 percent to 8.36 percent for employers with a negative ratio.

There is also the federal unemployment tax (FUTA). Per an Arizona Senate research brief, the FUTA rate is 6.0 percent on the first $7,000 of each worker's wages, with a credit of up to 5.4 percent for employers who pay state taxes on time, for a typical net rate of 0.6 percent.

Because the $8,000 state wage base is low, a high-turnover operation pays UI tax on a larger share of its total payroll. Ten people who each work two months can generate more taxable wages than two people who work the full year.

## How your rate is calculated

DES uses what it calls a **reserve ratio** system:

1. Your **reserve balance** is the UI taxes you have paid in, minus the unemployment benefits charged to your account.
2. That balance is divided by your **average annual taxable payroll** over up to three fiscal years.
3. The resulting ratio, positive or negative, places you in a rate group on the DES tax rate chart.

A higher ratio means a lower rate. **Benefit charges reduce your reserve,** so when former employees collect unemployment based on wages you paid, your ratio falls and your future rate can rise. DES also notes that statewide conditions matter: high unemployment can push rates up across the board, and low unemployment can bring them down.

Each year, DES sends a **Determination of Unemployment Tax Rate** notice showing your rate and the factors behind it, along with how to appeal if you think it is wrong.

## Why seasonal layoffs push your rate up

When you hire directly for peak and release those workers in January, some of them may file for unemployment. If their claims are based on wages you paid, the benefits can be charged to your account. Repeat that every year and your reserve ratio can drift downward, moving you into a higher rate group.

A few habits help:

- **Plan headcount carefully.** Hiring more people than you need and releasing them after a few weeks increases potential claims.
- **Respond to claim notices on time** and with accurate separation information, so DES has what it needs to decide each claim correctly.
- **Track your rate over several years,** not just one. A creeping rate is a signal to look at turnover and seasonal hiring practices.

## The voluntary payment option

Arizona allows employers to make a **voluntary contribution** to their UI account to raise their reserve ratio and potentially drop into a lower rate group. According to DES:

- The payment must be made by **February 28** to lower your rate for that calendar year.
- Your annual rate notice explains how to calculate the amount needed.
- Compare the payment to the tax savings before deciding. It only makes sense if the savings exceed the payment.

DES recommends paying by check, noting the purpose and sending it to the UI Tax Experience Rating Unit. Instructions are on the back of the rate notice. Mark your calendar for when the notice arrives so you have time to run the numbers.

## Where temporary staffing fits

When your seasonal workers come through a staffing agency, **the agency is the employer of record.** It pays the wages, pays the UI tax on those wages and handles unemployment claims from those workers. Their separations do not run through your UI account.

That is one reason many employers keep a stable core of direct employees and use temporary staffing for the peaks. Your own account reflects your steady workforce, while the swings are handled by the agency. The cost of that coverage is built into the bill rate, so compare it honestly against your own fully loaded cost, including UI, when you plan.

If you convert a temp to your direct payroll, that person becomes your employee, and future UI tax and any benefit charges based on your wages follow you from that point.

## A year-end UI checklist

1. **Watch for your rate notice** and check the figures against your records.
2. **Calculate whether a voluntary payment pays off** before February 28.
3. **Update your payroll system** with the new rate before the first 2027 payroll.
4. **Review last year's claims** and how quickly your team responded to DES notices.
5. **Plan 2027 seasonal hiring** with UI costs in mind: direct hires, temps or a mix.
