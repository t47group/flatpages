The day after Thanksgiving is the start of the busiest stretch of the year for many distribution centers. It is also a good time to plan the other end of peak: what happens when volume drops and seasonal workers leave in late December and January.

Arizona has specific rules about when final wages are due, and the deadline depends on whether the employee was let go or quit. Getting it wrong is not just a paperwork issue. Arizona law allows employees to recover up to three times unpaid wages in some cases. Here is what operations and HR leaders should know before the wind-down.

## The two deadlines

Arizona's final pay rule is A.R.S. 23-353.

**When you discharge an employee** (including ending employment because seasonal work is over), wages due must be paid **within seven working days or by the end of the next regular pay period, whichever is sooner.**

**When an employee quits,** wages are due **no later than the regular payday** for the pay period in which the employee left. The employee can ask to receive the payment by mail.

For a seasonal layoff, the first rule usually applies. If you are on a biweekly payroll and the next payday is ten working days away, the seven-working-day deadline comes first, which can mean an off-cycle check.

## How final pay must be delivered

The statute requires payment in a form that can be cashed immediately at face value, or by deposit into a financial institution the employee has chosen. Plan how you will deliver final checks to people who will not be returning to the site, and keep addresses and direct deposit information current through peak.

## What happens if pay is late

Two points worth knowing:

- **Treble damages.** Under A.R.S. 23-355, an employee who sues over wages the employer failed to pay may recover up to three times the unpaid amount.
- **State wage claims.** The Industrial Commission of Arizona (ICA) accepts unpaid wage claims of up to **$12,000**, filed within **one year** of when the claim accrued. Larger amounts go to the U.S. Department of Labor or to court. The ICA form notes that overtime, minimum wage, earned paid sick time and retaliation claims are handled separately.

## Paid sick time at separation

Arizona's earned paid sick time law affects the wind-down in two ways, according to the ICA's FAQs:

- **No payout is required.** Employers do not have to pay out unused earned paid sick time at separation, though they may choose to.
- **Rehire within nine months.** If you rehire an employee within nine months of separation, previously accrued, unused sick time generally must be **reinstated**, and the employee can use and accrue time right away. The ICA has said it will not enforce reinstatement against an employer that paid out the unused time at separation, if the employee accepted that payment.

That nine-month window matters for seasonal operations. Many of the people you release in January may be exactly the people you want back for next peak, or even for spring returns. Track separation dates and sick time balances so rehires are handled correctly.

## Plan the wind-down in steps

Here is the approach we recommend:

1. **Set your ramp-down calendar now.** Decide which weeks you expect volume to drop and how many people you will release each week.
2. **Map final pay deadlines to your payroll calendar.** For each release date, figure out whether you will hit the seven-working-day deadline on your regular payday or need an off-cycle run.
3. **Collect final timecards promptly.** Make sure supervisors approve the last week's hours quickly, including any missed punches.
4. **Include everything owed.** Final wages include regular pay, overtime and any earned bonuses. If you promised an end-of-peak attendance bonus, pay it if earned, and remember it affects the regular rate for overtime.
5. **Handle equipment returns carefully.** Arizona has specific rules about wage deductions. Talk to payroll or counsel before deducting for unreturned badges, scanners or uniforms.
6. **Identify your keepers.** Flag top performers for rehire, conversion or next season. Tell them you want them back.
7. **Record separation reasons accurately.** Records matter for unemployment claims and for future rehire decisions.

## How this works with temporary workers

When your seasonal labor comes through a staffing agency, the agency is the employer of record and handles final pay, sick time balances and unemployment paperwork. Ending an assignment on your site does not always end the worker's employment with the agency, which may place them elsewhere.

Your part is simpler but still important:

- **Give your agency advance notice** of release dates so it can plan reassignments and payroll.
- **Approve final hours quickly** so the agency can meet its own deadlines.
- **Tell the agency who you would like back** for future peaks or for conversion to direct hire.

This is one of the practical advantages of flexible staffing: your floor shrinks when volume does, without you running a dozen off-cycle payrolls in January.

## The bottom line

In Arizona, a discharged worker's final pay is due within seven working days or by the next regular payday, whichever comes first. Plan the timing alongside your ramp-down schedule, handle sick time and rehires correctly and keep in touch with the people you want back.
