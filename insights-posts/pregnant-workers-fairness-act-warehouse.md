Physical jobs raise practical questions when an employee is pregnant. Can she keep picking orders that require lifting? What if she needs to sit more often, drink water on the line or take extra restroom breaks? What about a lot porter who spends the day walking hot pavement?

Since June 2023, a federal law has answered those questions more directly than before: the **Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA)**. Many supervisors on warehouse floors and dealership lots still have not heard of it. Here is what it requires and how to handle requests well.

## Who the law covers

According to the EEOC, the PWFA took effect on **June 27, 2023**. It applies to private and public employers with **15 or more employees**, as well as employment agencies and labor organizations. The EEOC's implementing regulation took effect June 18, 2024.

The law requires covered employers to provide **reasonable accommodations** for a worker's **known limitations** related to pregnancy, childbirth or related medical conditions, unless the accommodation would cause an **undue hardship**, meaning significant difficulty or expense.

## What counts as a "known limitation"

The EEOC defines a limitation as a physical or mental condition related to pregnancy, childbirth or related conditions. It does not have to be severe. The EEOC gives examples like morning sickness and migraines, and notes that conditions can be minor or come and go. A limitation is "known" when the employee or their representative has communicated it to the employer.

No magic words are required. An employee saying "I'm pregnant and I can't lift those boxes right now" is enough to start the process.

## Accommodations that fit physical jobs

The EEOC lists examples of reasonable accommodations. Several map directly onto warehouse, auction and dealership work:

- **Additional, longer or more flexible breaks** to drink water, eat, rest or use the restroom
- **Allowing a water bottle or food** in areas where policy normally prohibits it
- **A stool or chair,** or the ability to alternate sitting and standing
- **Help with lifting** or light duty
- **Modified uniforms, dress code or properly fitting safety equipment**
- **Schedule changes,** shorter hours, part-time work or a later start time
- **Temporary reassignment** or temporary suspension of an essential job function
- **Leave** for medical appointments or recovery from childbirth

## The "temporarily unable" rule

One feature of the PWFA is different from the Americans with Disabilities Act. Under the PWFA, a worker can still be considered qualified even if she **temporarily cannot perform an essential function** of the job, as long as the inability is temporary, she could perform the function in the near future and the inability can be reasonably accommodated.

For a forklift operator or order picker, that may mean moving her to a less physical task for a period, such as scanning, labeling, cycle counts or returns processing, rather than taking her off the schedule.

## What employers must not do

The EEOC highlights several prohibited actions. Employers may not:

- Deny a reasonable accommodation that would not cause undue hardship
- Force a worker to accept an accommodation that was not arrived at through discussion with her
- Deny a job or opportunity because of the need for an accommodation
- **Require leave** when another accommodation would let her keep working
- Retaliate against someone for requesting an accommodation or reporting a violation

The leave point matters most in practice. Sending a pregnant employee home "for her own safety" when she could keep working with a modified task is the kind of decision the law was written to stop.

## Keep the process simple

The EEOC expects the interactive process to be quick in many cases, often a short conversation or email. A few practical guidelines:

- **Respond promptly.** Delays can themselves become a problem.
- **Limit documentation.** The EEOC says employers generally should not require medical documentation when the need is obvious or already known, or for common requests like extra restroom breaks, water or a stool. When documentation is appropriate, it should be limited to confirming the condition and the change needed.
- **Keep medical information confidential.**
- **Revisit as things change.** Needs often change at different stages of pregnancy and after childbirth.

## Related: lactation breaks

After the baby arrives, the federal PUMP Act separately requires reasonable break time and a private space other than a bathroom for nursing employees, for up to one year after birth. Plan the space in advance.

## When the worker is a temp

The PWFA applies to employment agencies as well as employers, and in practice both the staffing agency and the host site usually have a role. The agency receives the request and manages the employment relationship. The host controls the tasks, schedule and floor.

We suggest agreeing in advance with your staffing partner on:

- Who an assigned worker should tell when she needs an accommodation
- How quickly the host will respond about task changes or seating
- Which alternative tasks are available on site
- How confidentiality will be maintained

*This article is general information, not legal advice. The EEOC has signaled it may revisit parts of its PWFA regulation, though the law itself remains in effect. Confirm current obligations with employment counsel or the EEOC.*

## The bottom line

The Pregnant Workers Fairness Act asks employers to do something most good supervisors already want to do: find a practical way for a pregnant worker to keep working safely. Train your leads to recognize a request, respond quickly, avoid forcing leave and coordinate with your staffing partner when the worker is a temp.
