Every operations manager has heard a version of "the labor market is tight" or "the labor market is loosening." Those statements are only useful if you know what is behind them and how they apply to your building, your roles and your part of the Valley.

The good news is that some of the best labor market data available is free, public and updated regularly. Here is where to find it and how we suggest using it.

## The two main sources

### U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS)

The BLS produces the core federal labor statistics, many of them in partnership with state agencies. For Phoenix, the easiest starting point is the **[Phoenix-Mesa-Chandler Economy at a Glance](https://www.bls.gov/eag/eag.az_phoenix_msa.htm)** page, which pulls together the main metro-level series in one place.

### Arizona Office of Economic Opportunity (OEO)

The OEO is Arizona's state labor market information office. Its [labor market section](https://oeo.az.gov/labor-market) publishes industry employment and wages, occupational wages by county, unemployment and labor force trends for counties, cities and towns, and two-year and ten-year employment projections. The OEO also publishes a release schedule so you know when new numbers are coming.

## The data series worth knowing

You do not need to become an economist. A handful of series will cover most planning needs.

### Local Area Unemployment Statistics (LAUS)

LAUS provides labor force, employment and unemployment rates for states, metro areas, counties and many cities. It is the source of the metro unemployment rate you see in the news. For example, the BLS reported Phoenix-Mesa-Chandler unemployment at **4.9 percent** for August 2026 (preliminary, not seasonally adjusted).

**How to use it:** Unemployment trends give a broad sense of how many people are looking for work. A rising rate may make hiring easier; a falling one usually means more competition for workers.

### Current Employment Statistics (CES)

CES measures payroll jobs by industry, including **Trade, Transportation and Utilities**, **Manufacturing** and **Construction**, at the metro level, every month.

**How to use it:** Watch the industries that compete with you for workers. If transportation and warehousing employment is growing fast, expect more competition for warehouse labor. If construction is booming, outdoor and general labor roles may get harder to fill.

### Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS)

OEWS reports employment and wages for hundreds of specific occupations, such as laborers and freight movers, industrial truck operators and packers, at the state and metro level. It is published annually.

**How to use it:** OEWS is a solid benchmark for setting pay ranges. Compare your rates to the metro median and percentiles for the roles you hire. Remember it lags; the data reflects a reference period before publication, so adjust for wage growth since then.

### Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW)

QCEW counts employment and wages from unemployment insurance records, covering nearly all employers. It is very detailed by industry and county, but it is published with a lag of several months.

**How to use it:** Use QCEW to see longer-term industry trends in Maricopa County and to check average wages in your specific industry.

### Consumer Price Index (CPI)

The BLS publishes CPI nationally and for the Phoenix area. The national CPI-U also drives Arizona's annual minimum wage adjustment under state law.

**How to use it:** Inflation affects what workers need to earn and how they view your pay. It also tells you in September what the next January minimum wage will be.

## Turning data into decisions

Numbers are only useful if they change what you do. Here is how we suggest folding them into planning:

1. **Set a calendar.** Check LAUS and CES monthly, OEWS when the annual release comes out and CPI each September for the minimum wage.
2. **Pick your comparison set.** Track the two or three industries and five or so occupations that matter most to you. Ignore the rest.
3. **Benchmark pay once a year, at minimum.** Use OEWS as a baseline and supplement it with what competitors are advertising locally.
4. **Compare with your own metrics.** Applicant flow, time to fill, show rates and early turnover are your real-time labor market data. Public data explains the trend; your data tells you how it hits your operation.
5. **Plan scenarios.** If unemployment rises a point, what happens to your applicant pool? If a major employer opens nearby, what does that do to your pay rate? Rough scenarios beat no plan.
6. **Share it with your staffing partner.** A good agency sees applicant behavior across many clients and can add context to what the public data shows.

## Common pitfalls

- **Comparing seasonally adjusted and unadjusted numbers.** Metro data is often not seasonally adjusted, so compare the same month year over year.
- **Overreacting to one month.** Monthly estimates are revised. Look at the trend over several months.
- **Treating the metro as one market.** Labor conditions in the West Valley, East Valley and north Phoenix can differ. Commute patterns matter.
- **Using stale pay benchmarks.** A wage survey from two years ago may be well below today's market.

## The bottom line

Reliable labor market data for Phoenix is free and easy to access once you know where to look. Combine BLS and OEO data with your own hiring metrics, and you will make better decisions on pay, timing and headcount.
