Walk through a new distribution center in the West Valley today and you are likely to see some mix of conveyors, sortation, goods-to-person systems, autonomous mobile robots or automated storage. Ask the general manager whether that means fewer people, and the answer is usually more complicated than yes or no.

Here is our practical view of how automation is changing labor needs in Phoenix-area warehouses and how to plan around it.

## Automation changes the work more than it removes it

In most facilities, automation targets specific bottlenecks: walking time in picking, manual sorting, repetitive lifting or inventory counts. When those tasks are automated, the jobs around them change.

Typical shifts we see:

- **Less walking, more station work.** Goods-to-person systems bring product to a picker, so the job becomes faster-paced and more repetitive at a fixed station.
- **New exception handling.** When a robot or sorter cannot process an item, a person has to resolve it. Exception handling takes judgment and system knowledge.
- **More technical roles.** Maintenance technicians, controls specialists and system operators become critical, and they are harder to hire.
- **Different physical demands.** Some heavy-lift tasks go away, while others, like induction onto conveyors, stay manual and can be fast and repetitive.

## What usually stays manual

Even in highly automated buildings, several kinds of work remain people-driven:

- **Unloading floor-loaded trailers**, especially with mixed or irregular freight
- **Handling odd-sized, fragile or high-value items**
- **Returns processing**, where every item needs inspection and a decision
- **Kitting, value-added services and custom packing**
- **Quality checks and problem solving**
- **Cleaning, replenishment and general facility support**

Automation also tends to handle the steady baseline well but has fixed capacity. When volume spikes beyond what the system can process, operations often fall back on manual processes and additional labor to keep up.

## Peaks still need people

This is one of the most important planning points. A sortation system rated for a certain throughput does not stretch on Cyber Monday. Many automated sites still bring on seasonal workers for peak, but they may need them in different roles: induction, exception handling, manual overflow lines and trailer unloading, rather than traditional walking pick paths.

## The skills gap is real

Automation raises the bar for certain roles. Workers who interact with automated systems need to:

- Follow on-screen instructions and scan-based workflows accurately
- Recognize and report system faults
- Work safely around moving equipment and robots
- Adapt when processes change after a software update or system adjustment

Meanwhile, maintenance and controls technicians can be hard to find. In Phoenix, distribution centers often compete for those skill sets with the region's growing manufacturing and semiconductor employers.

## How to plan your workforce around automation

If your facility is adding automation, or you are evaluating it, here is how we would approach workforce planning:

1. **Map tasks, not headcount.** List every task in your process and mark which will be automated, which will change and which will stay manual. Headcount follows from that.
2. **Plan for the transition period.** Go-lives rarely hit full throughput on day one. Budget for extra labor while systems ramp up and teams learn new processes.
3. **Identify your exception handlers.** Pick people with good judgment and system aptitude and train them early.
4. **Invest in technician pipelines.** Consider apprenticeships, partnerships with local community colleges or upskilling promising associates into maintenance roles.
5. **Rethink your flex labor profile.** Tell your staffing partner which roles will need flexible labor after automation. The profile may shift from general pickers toward induction, unloading and returns.
6. **Update safety training.** Robots and conveyors introduce new hazards: pinch points, traffic interaction and lockout/tagout. Everyone on site, including temps, needs that training.
7. **Measure the right things.** Automated sites often track system uptime, exceptions per hour and throughput per labor hour rather than traditional pick rates.

## What this means for temporary labor

Automation does not eliminate the need for flexible labor; it reshapes it. Automated sites tend to want temps who are:

- Comfortable with scanners and screen-based instructions
- Able to maintain pace at a fixed station
- Safety conscious around equipment
- Reliable enough to train once and bring back for each peak

That last point matters. Training on automated systems takes time, so a returning pool of experienced seasonal workers is worth more at an automated site than at a manual one.

## The bottom line

Automation is spreading through Phoenix distribution, but it is changing jobs more than eliminating them. The facilities that handle the shift well plan by task, invest in technical skills and keep a flexible, trained workforce for the work machines do not cover.
