Peak season fills a building with new faces fast. A lot of them are temporary, many are new to the site and some are more comfortable in Spanish or another language than in English. That puts pressure on one of the least glamorous parts of safety: training. A signed attendance sheet from a 20-minute English-only video does not mean anyone learned anything.

OSHA has a clear position on this, and it is worth revisiting before your next onboarding wave.

## The rule: training has to be understood

OSHA's list of employer responsibilities includes a line that surprises some managers: **provide employee safety training in a language and vocabulary employees can understand.**

OSHA spelled this out in an April 2010 policy memo to its regional offices. The memo says that whenever an OSHA standard requires training or instruction, OSHA interprets that to mean the information must be presented in a manner employees are capable of understanding. Key points from the memo:

- **Language.** If an employee does not speak or understand English, training must be provided in a language the employee understands. Employers may also offer English instruction, but it does not replace training the worker can actually follow.
- **Vocabulary.** Training must account for an employee's limited vocabulary. Technical jargon can be as much of a barrier as a different language.
- **Literacy.** Handing written material to someone who cannot read it does not satisfy the training requirement.
- **Consistency.** If you routinely give work instructions in a particular language or at a particular vocabulary level, OSHA expects safety training to be delivered the same way.

## What inspectors look for

The memo directs compliance officers to go beyond checking whether training records exist. They are told to verify that training was presented in a form employees could understand. If an officer finds that training was not conveyed in an understandable way, the memo says the violation may be cited as **serious**.

The memo also notes that an employer may not rely on a work rule it never communicated in a language the employee could understand. In other words, "we told them" only counts if they could understand what you said.

Arizona runs its own state OSHA plan through ADOSH, part of the Industrial Commission of Arizona. State plans are required to be at least as effective as federal OSHA, so plan on the same expectation here.

## Temp workers: whose job is it?

Training is one of the areas where staffing agencies and host employers share responsibility. OSHA's guidance on temporary workers says, in general:

- The **staffing agency** is responsible for making sure its workers are properly trained, and needs a reasonable basis to believe the host's site-specific training covers the hazards workers will face.
- The **host employer** usually provides workplace-specific training, because it knows and controls the hazards on its floor.
- On **hazard communication**, the host has the primary role, since it controls the chemicals and hazards; the agency must at least inform its workers about the standard's requirements.

OSHA recommends that both parties put these duties in writing. The language question belongs in that conversation. If your site training is English-only and the crew coming in is mostly Spanish-speaking, both companies have a problem.

## Practical ways to get it right

You do not need a professional translation department. You need a process that confirms people understood.

1. **Know your crew.** Ask at onboarding which language each person is most comfortable with. Your staffing partner should already know this for the workers it sends.
2. **Train in that language.** Use bilingual trainers or leads for live instruction. OSHA publishes many resources in Spanish that can supplement your site material.
3. **Keep the vocabulary plain.** "Stay out of the forklift lane" beats "maintain separation from powered industrial truck travel paths."
4. **Show, don't just tell.** Walk the floor. Point out the emergency exits, eyewash stations, pedestrian lanes and lockout points. Demonstrate the safe way to lift, cut stretch wrap or work a dock plate.
5. **Check understanding.** Ask people to explain or demonstrate back. Some OSHA standards already require employers to verify that employees learned the material.
6. **Match signage.** If training is bilingual, make key signs and labels bilingual too.
7. **Document it.** Record the language used, who trained, what was covered and how understanding was checked. That record is far more useful than a signature alone.
8. **Repeat it.** Peak brings new people every week. Build short refreshers into shift starts instead of front-loading everything on day one.

## Why this pays off

Clear training is not only about avoiding citations. New workers have the least experience with your hazards, and peak volume leaves little room for error. When people understand the rules, they follow them more consistently, they ask questions sooner and supervisors spend less time correcting the same mistakes.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Consult your safety professional or counsel about your specific obligations.*

## The bottom line

OSHA expects safety training to be understood, not just delivered. Train in a language and vocabulary your crew can follow, check that it landed, document how you did it and agree in writing with your staffing partner on who covers what.
