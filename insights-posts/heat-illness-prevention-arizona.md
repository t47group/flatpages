October brings some relief to the Valley, which makes it the right time to look back at the summer and plan for the next one. Heat illness prevention is no longer just a best practice in Arizona. Between new state guidelines, an active state emphasis program and a renewed federal enforcement push, employers with warehouse floors, auction lots, dealership lots and outdoor crews are being watched more closely than ever.

Here is where things stand as of fall 2026.

## There is still no federal heat standard

Federal OSHA published a proposed heat injury and illness prevention rule in August 2024. The comment periods have closed, but the rule has not been finalized, and no final action date has been set.

That does not mean heat is unregulated. OSHA can and does cite employers for serious heat hazards under the **General Duty Clause** of the Occupational Safety and Health Act, which requires employers to keep workplaces free from recognized hazards likely to cause death or serious harm.

## OSHA renewed its heat emphasis program in 2026

OSHA's National Emphasis Program on outdoor and indoor heat-related hazards first launched in 2022. In April 2026, OSHA issued an updated version (CPL 03-00-024) that runs for five more years. Key points from the update:

- **Heat priority days** are days with a heat index of **80 degrees F or higher**. On those days, inspectors on site for other reasons are expected to ask about the employer's heat program.
- OSHA targets random heat inspections at establishments in designated high-hazard industries on days when the National Weather Service has issued a heat warning or advisory.
- Inspectors are expected to look for heat hazards when there is evidence of them, such as injury logs, employee statements or what they can see on site.

## What Arizona is doing

Arizona runs its own state OSHA plan through the **Arizona Division of Occupational Safety and Health (ADOSH)**, part of the Industrial Commission of Arizona (ICA).

### The Heat Stress State Emphasis Program

ADOSH launched a Heat Stress State Emphasis Program in July 2023. Under it, inspectors focus on heat hazards during inspections where heat-related illness could occur, including programmed inspections on high-heat days in higher-risk industries. ADOSH has emphasized five core elements:

- Adequate, cool drinking water
- Rest breaks as needed
- Shaded areas for recovery
- Training on heat stress signs and symptoms
- Acclimatization for new and returning workers

### The 2026 Workplace Heat Safety Guidelines

In May 2025, Governor Hobbs signed an executive order creating a Workplace Heat Safety Task Force. The task force delivered its recommendations at the end of 2025, covering water, shade, rest, acclimatization, training and best practices for written Heat Illness Prevention Plans. On **April 9, 2026**, the Industrial Commission voted to approve and implement those recommendations, which feed into an updated emphasis program.

According to the ICA, these guidelines are **not a new standard or rule**. But employers can still be cited when heat conditions are not adequately addressed and are likely to cause death or serious harm. ADOSH also offers a downloadable heat plan template, which is a good starting point for smaller operations. Find it at [azica.gov/heat](https://azica.gov/heat).

## Heat is not only an outdoor problem

Warehouse managers sometimes assume heat rules are about roofers and landscapers. In Phoenix, a dock door open to a 112-degree afternoon, a mezzanine with limited airflow or a trailer being unloaded in July can all create serious heat exposure. The same goes for auction lot drivers, porters and detail crews working on hot pavement.

## A practical heat plan for 2027

Use the cooler months to get your program in writing. A solid plan typically covers:

1. **Water.** Where it is, who keeps it stocked and how often workers are encouraged to drink.
2. **Rest and shade.** Cool-down areas close to the work, and a culture where taking a break is expected, not frowned upon.
3. **Acclimatization.** New and returning workers need time to build heat tolerance. Plan reduced exposure in their first days, especially early in the season.
4. **Training.** Everyone, including supervisors and temps, should recognize heat exhaustion and heat stroke and know what to do.
5. **Monitoring.** Track heat index and adjust work, such as moving heavy tasks to early shifts.
6. **Emergency response.** Who calls 911, how to cool someone quickly and how to direct responders to your site.
7. **Records.** Document training, incidents and plan reviews.

## Temps and heat: shared responsibility

OSHA's position is that staffing agencies and host employers are **jointly responsible** for temporary workers' safety. In practice, the host controls the work environment, while the agency should confirm the host has proper protections in place and that its workers are trained.

Temps are also often the newest people on site, which means they are the least acclimatized. When you bring on a crew for a summer surge, acclimatization planning should apply to them from day one. We recommend agreeing in writing on who provides heat training, who supplies water and cooling, and how heat-related concerns are reported.

*This is general information, not legal advice. Consult your safety professional or counsel about your specific obligations.*

## The bottom line

There is no single federal heat rule yet, but Arizona employers face active state and federal enforcement and clear expectations from ADOSH. A written plan, built now and practiced before May, protects your people and your operation.
