The semiconductor build-out in north Phoenix is the largest industrial project most of us will see in our careers. Even if your business has nothing to do with chips, it is changing the labor market you hire from. Construction crews, suppliers, logistics providers and service companies are all growing around it, and they are recruiting from the same Valley workforce you are.

Here is what is confirmed about the project and how we think about its effect on Phoenix-area employers.

## The scale of TSMC Arizona

According to TSMC's official Arizona site:

- TSMC's planned investment in Arizona has grown from an initial **$12 billion to $265 billion**, which the company describes as the largest foreign direct investment in a greenfield project in U.S. history.
- The full plan includes **six wafer fabs, two advanced packaging facilities and an R&D center.**
- **Fab 1** began high-volume production on its N4 process in the fourth quarter of 2024.
- **Fab 2** completed structural construction in 2025, with volume production targeted for the second half of 2027.
- **Fab 3** broke ground in April 2025.
- Construction on **Fab 4 and the first advanced packaging facility** began in early 2026.
- TSMC Arizona reports **more than 3,500 employees**, and the company expects the first three fabs to create about **6,000 direct high-tech jobs**, plus construction and supplier jobs.

In March 2025, when TSMC announced a $100 billion expansion of its U.S. plans, the company said that expansion was expected to support roughly **40,000 construction jobs over four years**.

## How a project this size affects the labor market

The direct fab jobs are mostly specialized: engineers, technicians and equipment specialists. The wider ripple effect is what matters for most Valley employers.

### Construction pulls from general labor

Multi-year construction of fabs and support facilities requires large crews: laborers, equipment operators, electricians, pipefitters and support staff. Construction support work can compete for the same people who might otherwise work in warehouses or on auction lots, particularly those who are comfortable with outdoor, physical work.

### Suppliers and logistics follow

Chip manufacturing relies on a network of chemical, gas, equipment and materials suppliers, plus the logistics companies that move those goods. As that network grows, it adds warehousing, distribution and transportation jobs in the region.

### Technicians are in demand everywhere

Fabs need maintenance and facilities technicians, and so do automated warehouses, manufacturers and data centers. Employers across industries are recruiting from a similar pool of mechanically skilled workers.

### Wage pressure spreads

When large employers raise pay for certain roles, nearby employers often feel it, especially those hiring for physically demanding or skilled hourly jobs. Even without precise data on the size of the effect, it is reasonable to expect continued competition for reliable hourly workers in the north and northwest Valley.

### Housing and commute patterns shift

New jobs bring new residents, new housing and more traffic on key corridors. Commute time is one of the biggest factors in whether hourly workers stay, so changes in where people live and how they travel can affect attendance and retention.

## What this means for warehouse, auction and service employers

You do not have to be in the chip business to plan around it. A few practical moves:

1. **Watch the north and northwest Valley closely.** If your operation sits along the I-17, Loop 303 or Deer Valley corridors, competition for hourly workers may be more intense than in other parts of the metro.
2. **Benchmark pay more often.** Annual pay reviews may not be enough when a major employer nearby is ramping up. Check competitive rates quarterly.
3. **Make your jobs easy to start and easy to stay in.** Clear schedules, predictable hours and good supervision help you compete without matching every dollar.
4. **Build a technician pipeline.** Upskilling promising associates into maintenance roles is often more reliable than hiring experienced technicians on the open market.
5. **Use flexible labor for surges.** A staffing partner with a broad pool can help cover peaks without overcommitting to permanent headcount in a tight market.
6. **Watch the data.** Track Phoenix employment and wage trends through BLS and the Arizona Office of Economic Opportunity to see how the market is moving.

## A longer-term opportunity

A major manufacturing anchor can also strengthen the regional economy over time: more training programs, more suppliers and more people moving to the Valley for work. Employers who invest in their own workforce now are better positioned to benefit from that growth rather than just compete with it.

## The bottom line

TSMC Arizona's build-out is reshaping the Phoenix labor market well beyond the fab gates. Expect steady competition for hourly and technical workers, especially in the north Valley, and plan pay, retention and flexible staffing accordingly.
