December is when safety managers start thinking about the OSHA 300 log. The year's records need to be reviewed and closed out, a summary has to go up on the wall by February 1, and many warehouse and distribution employers have to submit data to OSHA electronically by March 2.

There is one question that comes up every year at sites that use temporary labor: when a temp gets hurt, whose log does it go on? The answer surprises a lot of host employers.

## The rule: whoever supervises day to day records it

OSHA's recordkeeping regulation, 29 CFR 1904.31, says you must record injuries and illnesses of employees on your payroll **and** of workers who are not on your payroll if you **supervise them on a day-to-day basis**. It specifically covers workers provided by a temporary staffing service.

In other words, **if your leads direct the temp's daily work, the injury goes on your OSHA 300 log,** even though the staffing agency pays the worker and carries the workers' comp policy.

OSHA's interpretation letters add a few useful details:

- **Day-to-day supervision means directing the details, means, methods and processes of the work,** not just telling the agency what output you need.
- **Each case is recorded only once.** The staffing agency does not also record a case that goes on the host's log. The two companies should coordinate.
- **The facts on the floor control,** not the contract. A clause saying "the agency handles all recordkeeping" does not change who supervises the work.
- In one OSHA letter, an agency handled HR tasks like leave, benefits, drug screening and discipline, and even had on-site staff. OSHA said that was not day-to-day supervision. The host assigned the daily tasks, so the host had to record the injuries.

This is different from workers' compensation, where the agency as employer of record typically carries the coverage and handles the claim. Recordkeeping and comp follow different rules.

## What makes a case recordable

Not every scrape goes on the log. According to OSHA, a work-related injury or illness is recordable if it results in:

- Death
- Days away from work
- Restricted work or transfer to another job
- Medical treatment beyond first aid
- Loss of consciousness
- A significant injury or illness diagnosed by a physician or other licensed health care professional

Covered employers keep the OSHA 300 Log, the 301 Incident Reports and the 300A Annual Summary, and retain them for **five years**. Some smaller employers and certain low-hazard industries are partially exempt from routine recordkeeping; check OSHA's criteria if you are unsure.

## Year-end steps and deadlines

### Review and certify the 300A

Under 29 CFR 1904.32, after the year ends you must review the log for accuracy, fix errors and complete the Form 300A summary, including the average number of employees and total hours worked. A **company executive** must certify it: an owner, a corporate officer, the highest-ranking official at the establishment or that person's immediate supervisor.

If you supervised temps day to day and recorded their injuries, think about how their hours fit into your totals so the summary is consistent with the log. Ask your staffing partner for hours worked at your site; they have them on payroll.

### Post it from February 1 to April 30

The certified 300A must be posted where employee notices are usually posted, **from February 1 through April 30.** OSHA says this applies even if you had no recordable cases; you post a summary with zeros.

### Submit electronically by March 2

Under 29 CFR 1904.41, certain establishments must submit injury data to OSHA through its Injury Tracking Application by **March 2** of the following year:

- Establishments with **250 or more employees** that must keep records submit the 300A.
- Establishments with **20 to 249 employees** in designated industries submit the 300A. OSHA's list includes **warehousing and storage (NAICS 4931)** and **wholesale trade (NAICS 42)**.
- Establishments with **100 or more employees** in a separate list of designated industries must also submit 300 and 301 data.

OSHA notes that **part-time, seasonal and temporary workers count** toward these employee totals. A distribution center that ran a large peak crew in 2026 could cross a threshold it doesn't usually reach. Check OSHA's coverage tool if you're unsure.

### Report severe injuries right away

Separately from the log, serious events have to be reported quickly. ADOSH, Arizona's state OSHA program, requires employers to report work-related fatalities **within 8 hours** and in-patient hospitalizations, amputations and eye losses **within 24 hours**. ADOSH's reporting line is (602) 542-5795.

## Coordinate with your staffing partner

1. **Agree on the reporting path.** Temps should report injuries to their site lead and to the agency right away.
2. **Share incident information quickly** so whoever records the case has what they need within OSHA's timeframes.
3. **Confirm whose log it goes on,** based on who actually directs the work.
4. **Get temp hours by site** at year end for your 300A totals.
5. **Review the cases together.** Patterns in temp injuries often point to training or onboarding gaps you can fix before next peak.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Consult your safety professional or counsel about your specific recordkeeping obligations.*

## The bottom line

If your supervisors direct a temp's daily work, that temp's recordable injuries belong on your OSHA 300 log. Close out the year carefully, certify and post the 300A by February 1, submit by March 2 if you're covered and coordinate with your agency so every case is recorded once, correctly.
