Ask ten supervisors whether Arizona requires a lunch break and you will get several different answers. Many people assume the rules are like California's. They are not. But "not required" does not mean "anything goes." Federal law decides which breaks count as paid time, and a separate federal law protects break time for nursing employees.

During peak, when shifts run long and supervisors are juggling coverage, these details are easy to get wrong. Here is a clear rundown for Phoenix-area operations.

## Arizona does not require meal or rest breaks for adults

The U.S. Department of Labor maintains tables of state meal period and rest period laws for private-sector employers. **Arizona does not appear in either table** for adult workers. DOL's rest period table notes that states not listed do not require paid rest periods.

The federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) does not require lunch or coffee breaks either. So for adult employees in Arizona, whether to offer breaks, and how many, is generally up to the employer.

Most employers offer them anyway, for good reason. People working a 10-hour shift on a pick line or a hot auction lot need rest to stay safe and productive. And once you offer breaks, the federal rules below apply.

## Short breaks must be paid

DOL guidance is clear: **rest periods of short duration, usually 20 minutes or less, must be counted as hours worked.** That means:

- They must be paid.
- They count toward the 40-hour overtime threshold.
- You cannot clock people out for a 10- or 15-minute break and treat it as unpaid.

This catches employers who use automated timekeeping that deducts every punch-out. If someone punches out for a 12-minute break, that time still has to be paid.

## Meal periods can be unpaid, if they are real

A bona fide meal period, **typically 30 minutes or more,** generally does not need to be paid. The key condition, per DOL, is that the employee must be **completely relieved from duty** for the purpose of eating a regular meal.

That condition is where problems start. A meal period is not truly off duty if the employee is:

- Expected to watch the dock door or answer the radio
- Required to stay at a workstation
- Interrupted to help with a rush and then sent back to finish eating

If any of that is routine, the time may be compensable. Train supervisors to leave people alone during unpaid meals, or to pay the time when they cannot.

## Other time that often gets missed

DOL's hours-worked guidance also covers situations common in warehouses and lots:

- **Waiting time.** If an employee is "engaged to wait," such as being told to stand by until a trailer arrives, that is work time.
- **On-call time on premises.** If you require someone to stay on site on call, that time counts.
- **Required training.** Training generally counts as work time unless it is outside normal hours, voluntary, not job-related and no other work is done during it.
- **The workday.** The workday runs from the first principal activity to the last, which may be longer than the scheduled shift.

## Lactation breaks are required

The **PUMP for Nursing Mothers Act** expanded federal break time protections in December 2022. According to DOL, nearly all FLSA-covered employees who need to express breast milk are entitled to:

- **Reasonable break time** each time they need to pump, for up to one year after the child's birth
- **A private space that is not a bathroom,** shielded from view and free from intrusion by coworkers and the public

On pay: pump breaks can be unpaid only if the employee is completely relieved from duty. If you pay other breaks, pump breaks taken during those breaks must be paid the same way, and any work done while pumping must be paid.

Employers with fewer than 50 employees can claim an exemption only if compliance would cause **undue hardship**, which is judged by significant difficulty or expense relative to the employer's size and resources. It is not an automatic small-business exemption.

For warehouses and dealerships, the space requirement takes some planning. A locking office, a converted storage room or a dedicated room near the floor can work. A restroom stall cannot.

## Minors are different

Some states set break rules for workers under 18, and federal law sets hours limits for younger teens. If you are bringing on 16- or 17-year-olds for winter break, check the youth rules separately. We cover those in a separate post.

## What we recommend

1. **Write a break policy** that says how many breaks, how long, and which are paid. Apply it to everyone on site, including temps.
2. **Pay all breaks of 20 minutes or less.** Check that your timekeeping system does not deduct them.
3. **Protect unpaid meals.** If supervisors regularly interrupt meals, either stop the interruptions or pay the time.
4. **Set up a lactation space** before anyone asks for it, and tell supervisors how to handle requests.
5. **Align with your staffing partner.** The agency pays temps, so it needs accurate punches and should know your break schedule. Make sure everyone's records match.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Confirm your specific obligations with your HR advisor or employment counsel.*

## The bottom line

Arizona leaves break schedules to employers, but federal law still decides what counts as paid time. Pay short breaks, keep meal periods truly off duty, provide lactation breaks and space, and make sure your timekeeping matches your policy.
