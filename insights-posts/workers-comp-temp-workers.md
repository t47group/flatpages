One of the first questions a new client asks us is some version of: "If one of your people gets hurt at my facility, whose workers' comp pays?" It is a smart question. The answer is usually simple, but the details around it matter, and they are worth understanding before an incident, not after.

This is a general explainer, not legal advice. Workers' compensation law is state specific and fact specific.

## The basics of workers' comp in Arizona

Arizona requires employers to secure workers' compensation coverage for their employees. Employers generally do this one of two ways:

- **Buying a policy** from an insurance carrier authorized to write workers' comp in Arizona
- **Self-insuring**, with approval and oversight from the Industrial Commission of Arizona (ICA)

Workers' comp is a no-fault system. An injured employee receives medical and wage-replacement benefits regardless of who caused the injury. In exchange, an employer that has properly secured coverage is generally protected from being sued in tort by the employee for that injury. This trade-off is commonly called the **exclusive remedy**.

The ICA processes and adjudicates workers' comp claims in Arizona, and its Special Fund steps in for workers whose employers failed to carry coverage.

## The typical arrangement with a staffing agency

In a standard temporary staffing relationship:

- **The staffing agency is the employer of record.** It recruits, hires, pays and issues the W-2.
- **The agency carries the workers' compensation policy** covering its employees, including those on assignment at your site.
- **The client (host employer) directs the day-to-day work** and controls the physical workplace.

So in the normal case, when a temp is injured on assignment, the claim goes through the **agency's** workers' comp coverage. That is a big part of what you are paying for in the bill rate: the agency carries the payroll taxes, the comp premium and the claims administration.

## Where it gets more nuanced

Workers' comp and liability questions around temporary workers can turn on specific facts. Courts in many states, including Arizona, have looked at concepts like "special employer" or "borrowed employee" status, which can treat a host employer as an employer of the temporary worker for certain purposes. Depending on the facts and the contract, that can affect questions like whether the host is also protected by the exclusive remedy, or whether the host could face a separate claim.

We are not going to try to summarize case law here. The practical takeaway is that the contract between you and your staffing agency, and the way the work is actually supervised, both matter. Have your counsel and your insurance broker review the arrangement.

## Safety responsibility is shared

Separate from who pays a claim, there is the question of who is responsible for preventing injuries. OSHA's position is direct: **staffing agencies and host employers are jointly responsible** for maintaining a safe workplace for temporary workers. OSHA expects:

- Host employers to treat temporary workers like any other workers on site, including training and hazard protection
- Staffing agencies to inquire about and verify that the host has met its safety responsibilities
- Both parties to clearly communicate and define responsibilities, ideally in their contract

In practice, the host is in the best position to provide site-specific training (equipment, layout, hazards), while the agency handles general safety orientation and confirms the assignment is appropriate.

## What should be in your staffing agreement

A good agreement removes guesswork. Look for clear language on:

1. **Proof of coverage.** Ask for a current certificate of insurance showing the agency's workers' comp and general liability coverage.
2. **Scope of work.** Define the job duties temps will perform. If a temp is hired for picking and packing, you should not have them operate a forklift without the agency's knowledge and proper certification.
3. **Training responsibilities.** Who provides general safety orientation and who provides site-specific and equipment training.
4. **PPE.** Who supplies it, and which items.
5. **Injury reporting.** How quickly the host notifies the agency, who arranges care, and who completes the required reports.
6. **Return to work.** Whether the host can offer modified duty to an injured temp, which can help both the worker and claim costs.
7. **Indemnification and insurance terms.** Have counsel review these.

## Keeping claims down in the first place

The most expensive claim is the one that should not have happened. A few habits make a difference:

- **Match the person to the job.** Assignments that involve lifting, heat or equipment need the right candidates.
- **Never change duties on the fly.** Scope creep is a common source of temp injuries. If the work changes, tell the agency first.
- **Orient on day one.** A 15-minute site walk covering exits, traffic lanes, dock edges and break areas prevents a lot of problems.
- **Report near misses.** They are free lessons.
- **Report injuries promptly.** Delays make claims harder for everyone, including the worker.

## The bottom line

In a typical staffing relationship, the agency employs the worker and carries their workers' comp. But the host controls the worksite, shares safety responsibility, and should have a clear written agreement covering coverage, duties and reporting.
