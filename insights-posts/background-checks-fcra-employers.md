Background checks are routine for a lot of the roles we fill. Dealerships want them for lot drivers and porters who handle keys and vehicles. Distribution centers want them for people who work around high-value inventory. Auction clients want them for anyone moving customer cars. The check itself is usually quick. The paperwork around it is where employers get into trouble.

When you use an outside company to run a background check, a federal law called the **Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)** applies, and the Federal Trade Commission has published clear guidance on what employers have to do. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) adds a second layer: how you use the results. Here is a practical overview.

## When the FCRA applies

The FCRA covers "consumer reports" prepared by a consumer reporting agency, which includes most third-party background screening companies. If you pay a screening vendor to pull criminal records, verify employment or compile other background information for a hiring decision, assume the FCRA applies and follow its steps.

## Before you run the check

According to the FTC, an employer must do three things before getting a consumer report on an applicant or employee:

1. **Give a written disclosure in a stand-alone document.** The notice that you may use a report for employment decisions cannot be buried inside the job application. It has to stand on its own.
2. **Get written authorization.** The person must give permission in writing. The authorization can be on the same document as the disclosure. If you want the permission to cover checks throughout employment, the FTC says that should be stated clearly and conspicuously.
3. **Certify to the screening company.** You confirm to the vendor that you gave notice, got permission, will follow the FCRA and will not misuse the information in violation of equal opportunity laws.

There are extra notice requirements for "investigative" reports, which are based on personal interviews about someone's character or reputation. Most standard criminal and employment checks are not investigative reports, but confirm with your vendor which type you are ordering.

## Before you say no: the pre-adverse action notice

This is the step that gets skipped most often. If something in the report might lead you to not hire someone, withdraw an offer or take other adverse action, the FTC says you must first give the person:

- A notice that includes a **copy of the report** you relied on
- A copy of **"A Summary of Your Rights Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act"**

The point is to give the person a chance to review the report and dispute anything inaccurate before the decision is final. Records do get mixed up, especially with common names. A short pause here protects both of you.

## After the decision: the adverse action notice

Once you make a final decision, you must notify the person orally, in writing or electronically. Per the FTC, that notice must include:

- The name, address and phone number of the screening company that supplied the report
- A statement that the screening company did not make the decision and cannot explain why it was made
- Notice of the person's right to dispute the accuracy or completeness of the information, and to get a free additional report from the company if they ask within 60 days

## How you use the results: EEOC guidance

The FCRA governs the process. Federal anti-discrimination law governs how you use what you learn. The EEOC's guidance for employers makes a few points that matter for high-volume hourly hiring:

- **Apply the same standards to everyone.** Rejecting one applicant for a record while hiring another with a similar record is evidence of discrimination.
- **Watch for disparate impact.** A policy that screens out people with certain records can be unlawful if it disproportionately excludes a protected group and is not job related and consistent with business necessity.
- **Tie the standard to the job.** A recent theft conviction may be relevant for someone handling inventory or vehicle keys. The same record may say much less about someone sorting returns. Think about what the role actually involves.
- **Consider disability-related explanations.** If a background problem was caused by a disability, the EEOC says to give the person a chance to show they can still do the job, unless that would cause significant difficulty or expense.

## Keep and dispose of records properly

The EEOC says employers generally must keep application and hiring records, including those for people not hired, for one year from when they were made or the personnel action was taken, whichever is later. If a discrimination charge is filed, keep records until the matter is resolved.

When the retention period ends, the FTC's disposal rule requires secure destruction: shredding, burning or pulverizing paper, and destroying electronic files so they cannot be read or reconstructed.

## A note on driving roles

For lot drivers, porters and auction drivers, many employers also review driving history. If a screening company supplies that driving record, treat it like any other report from that vendor and follow the same notice and authorization steps. Set your driving standards in writing before you start reviewing records, and apply them consistently.

## How this works when you use a staffing agency

When temporary workers come through an agency, the agency is the employer of record and typically orders the checks, collects the authorizations and handles the notices. As the client, you usually define the screening standard for your site.

A few questions worth asking any staffing partner, including us:

1. **Who orders the check, and which screening company is used?**
2. **What exactly is included** (counties, years of history, driving record, employment verification)?
3. **Do you send pre-adverse and adverse action notices** every time, with the required documents?
4. **Is our screening standard written down,** and is it applied the same way to every candidate?
5. **What happens when we convert a temp to direct hire?** If you want your own check at conversion, you will need your own disclosure and authorization.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Background check rules can also vary by state and city, so confirm your obligations with employment counsel.*

## The bottom line

A background check takes a few days. A sloppy process can create exposure that lasts years. Use a stand-alone disclosure, get written authorization, send both notices before and after any adverse decision, and apply a written, job-related standard to every candidate.
