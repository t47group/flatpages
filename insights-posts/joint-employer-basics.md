"Joint employer" is one of those phrases that makes operations managers nervous, usually because they have heard it in the context of a lawsuit. It does not need to be scary. Understanding the basic idea helps you use temporary labor confidently and keep the roles between you and your staffing partner clear.

This is a general overview. It is not legal advice, and the rules vary by law and by situation.

## What joint employment means

In a staffing relationship, two companies have a hand in one worker's job:

- **The staffing agency** recruits, hires, pays, withholds taxes, carries workers' comp and issues the W-2.
- **The client, or host employer,** decides what work gets done and supervises it on site.

Joint employment is the idea that, depending on the facts, **both companies can be considered employers of the same worker** under certain laws. When that happens, both can share responsibility for compliance with those laws.

The important word is "certain." Different laws use different tests. The question is often asked separately for wage and hour law, labor relations law, workplace safety and anti-discrimination law.

## Where the federal rules stand in 2026

The federal rules have changed several times over the past decade. Here is the current picture as of October 2026.

### Labor relations (NLRB)

The National Labor Relations Board issued a broader joint employer rule in 2023, but a federal court vacated it in March 2024 before it took effect. In February 2026, the NLRB formally withdrew the 2023 rule and restored the text of its **2020 standard**, which generally focuses on whether a company has substantial direct and immediate control over essential terms of employment such as wages, benefits, hours, hiring, discharge and supervision.

### Wage and hour (DOL)

In April 2026, the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division proposed a new rule on joint employer status under the Fair Labor Standards Act, the Family and Medical Leave Act and the Migrant and Seasonal Agricultural Worker Protection Act. The comment period closed in June 2026. **As of this writing, the proposal has not been finalized.** If and when it is, the standard for wage and hour purposes could become more uniform nationwide.

### Workplace safety (OSHA)

OSHA takes a practical view: staffing agencies and host employers **share responsibility** for temporary workers' safety. Each party is expected to address the hazards it is best positioned to control. That is less a legal test than an operating expectation.

## What this means day to day

For most employers, the takeaway is not to avoid staffing agencies. It is to understand that **how you manage temps on your floor matters**, and to keep each party's role clear. A few areas to think about:

### Supervision and direction

Directing the work is a normal part of using temporary labor. Your leads will assign tasks, set the pace and give feedback. That is expected.

### Hiring, firing and discipline

These are areas where clear roles help. A common, sensible practice: the host tells the agency when a worker is not a fit for the assignment, and the agency handles the employment decision and any discipline. Avoid having your supervisors "fire" temps directly.

### Pay and hours

The agency pays the worker. Make sure hours are recorded accurately and approved promptly, including any time spent in pre-shift meetings, security screening or required training. Unpaid off-the-clock time is a common source of wage claims, and in a joint employment scenario the host may share exposure.

### Workplace conduct

Harassment and discrimination policies should apply to everyone on your floor. Make sure temps know how to report a concern, and make sure your supervisors know to pass reports to the agency right away.

### Safety

Site-specific training, equipment and PPE are typically the host's job. General orientation and confirming the assignment is appropriate are typically the agency's. Put it in writing.

## Questions to settle in your staffing agreement

1. Who handles hiring, discipline and termination decisions?
2. How are hours captured, approved and corrected?
3. Who provides which training, and who supplies PPE?
4. How are complaints and safety concerns reported and escalated?
5. What are the insurance and indemnification terms? (Have counsel review these.)
6. How does conversion to direct hire work?

## A word on co-employment worries

Some employers worry that any involvement with a temp makes them the employer for every purpose. That is not how it works. The answers depend on the specific law and the actual facts. The best protection is a clear agreement, consistent practices and a staffing partner that handles its employer responsibilities well.

## The bottom line

Joint employment is a legal concept, but it is managed through everyday habits: clear roles, accurate time records, consistent conduct policies and good communication with your agency. The federal rules are still evolving, so revisit your practices periodically.
