When schools let out for winter break, a lot of teenagers want work, and a lot of employers still need hands for holiday peak and post-holiday returns. Hiring a 16- or 17-year-old for a few weeks can be a good fit for both. But the rules for minors are stricter than many supervisors expect, and some of the most common tasks in warehouses and on car lots are off-limits until 18.

Child labor violations can bring civil money penalties, and DOL says those can be higher when a violation contributes to a minor's injury and doubled for willful or repeated violations. Here is what to know before you put a teenager on the schedule.

## Federal and Arizona rules both apply

Both the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) and Arizona law regulate youth employment. The Industrial Commission of Arizona (ICA) notes that when both apply, **the more restrictive requirement controls.**

## Hours: 16 and 17 vs. 14 and 15

Under federal law, **16- and 17-year-olds have no limits on hours or times of day.** They can work full shifts during winter break in any job that is not declared hazardous. Confirm with the ICA whether any Arizona-specific rules apply to your situation.

Younger teens are a different story. Under DOL rules, 14- and 15-year-olds may work only outside school hours, no more than 3 hours on a school day, 18 hours in a school week, 8 hours on a non-school day and 40 hours in a non-school week, and generally only between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m. outside the summer. More importantly for our industries, the ICA lists **warehousing** among occupations prohibited for workers 15 and under. In practice, warehouse and lot employers should plan on hiring 16 and older.

## Jobs that are off-limits until 18

Federal Hazardous Occupations Orders (HOs) bar workers under 18 from certain jobs no matter how many hours they work. The ones that matter most for warehouses, dealerships and auctions:

### Forklifts and other lifting equipment (HO 7)

Workers under 18 may not operate, ride on or assist with most power-driven hoisting equipment. According to DOL, that includes **forklifts**, skid-steers, manlifts, **scissor lifts** and cranes. A 17-year-old can pick, pack and stage freight by hand, but should not be on a forklift, order picker or reach truck, even "just to move one pallet."

DOL does carve out electric or pneumatic lifts used to raise cars in garages and service stations, which is relevant for some dealership service departments. Read the order carefully before relying on it.

### Balers and compactors (HO 12)

Minors may not operate balers or compactors. Under specific conditions, 16- and 17-year-olds may load, but not operate or unload, certain scrap paper balers and paper box compactors. In a distribution center with cardboard balers on every dock, set a clear rule that minors stay away from them.

### Power saws (HO 14)

Operating or helping with power-driven circular, band, chain and reciprocating saws is prohibited for minors.

### Driving (HO 2)

This is the big one for auction and dealership employers. Under federal law:

- **16-year-olds may not drive on public roads** as part of their job.
- **17-year-olds may drive only in narrow circumstances.** Per DOL Fact Sheet #34, the driving must be occasional and incidental (no more than one-third of the workday and 20 percent of the workweek), in daylight, in a car or small truck rated at 6,000 pounds or less, with seat belts, within 30 miles of the workplace, by a teen who has a valid license, completed driver education and had no moving violations at hire.
- Even then, 17-year-olds **may not tow vehicles, make urgent time-sensitive deliveries, do route deliveries** or carry more than three passengers.

Arizona adds its own limits on young drivers. The ICA's guidance describes total driving time limits and a cap on daily driving distance for 16- and 17-year-olds, and also notes the federal restriction. Because the more restrictive rule controls, most dealership and auction employers simply keep minors out of driving roles.

The DOL fact sheet focuses on public roads. If you are thinking about having a minor move cars on a private lot, get advice first.

## Practical guidelines for winter-break hires

1. **Verify age before the first shift.** Keep proof of age in the file.
2. **Write down the prohibited tasks** for each minor's role and give a copy to the supervisor.
3. **Badge or flag minors** so leads know at a glance who cannot operate equipment.
4. **Train in plain language.** New, young workers need clear instruction on what they can and cannot touch.
5. **Assign appropriate work.** Hand picking, packing, labeling, cleaning, staging and returns processing are often good fits, as long as no prohibited equipment is involved.
6. **Watch for "helping out."** Most child labor violations on the floor happen when a busy lead asks a minor to jump on equipment for a minute.

## Temps under 18

Many staffing agencies set their own minimum ages for certain assignments, often 18 for any role involving equipment or driving. If you want to include 16- or 17-year-olds in a seasonal crew, discuss it with your agency first so both sides understand which tasks are allowed.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Youth employment rules have many details and exceptions. Confirm your specific obligations with the ICA, DOL or employment counsel.*

## The bottom line

Teens on winter break can help cover peak and returns, but forklifts, balers, power saws and most driving are off-limits until 18. Hire 16 and older for warehouse work, define the allowed tasks in writing and make sure every supervisor knows the rules.
