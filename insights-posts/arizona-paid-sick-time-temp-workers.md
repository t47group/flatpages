Arizona voters approved Proposition 206, the Fair Wages and Healthy Families Act, in 2016. Along with the minimum wage increases most people remember, it created a statewide earned paid sick time requirement that took effect July 1, 2017. Nearly a decade later, we still field questions from operations and HR managers about how it works, especially when their workforce is a mix of direct employees and temps.

Here are the basics.

## Who is covered

Arizona's earned paid sick time law applies to most private employers in the state, regardless of size. The Industrial Commission of Arizona (ICA) enforces it, with rules in Title 20, Chapter 5, Article 12 of the Arizona Administrative Code. Full-time, part-time and temporary employees are all generally covered.

## How accrual works

The core rules come from A.R.S. 23-372:

- **Accrual rate:** Employees accrue at least **one hour of paid sick time for every 30 hours worked.**
- **Annual cap, 15 or more employees:** Employees are not entitled to accrue or use more than **40 hours** per year, unless the employer sets a higher limit.
- **Annual cap, fewer than 15 employees:** The cap is **24 hours** per year, unless the employer sets a higher limit.
- **When accrual starts:** At the start of employment.
- **Waiting period:** An employer may require new employees to wait until the **90th calendar day** of employment before using accrued time.
- **Carryover:** Unused time carries over to the next year, subject to the annual use cap. Alternatively, an employer can pay out unused time at year end and front-load enough time at the start of the next year to meet the requirements.

The law defines a "year" as a regular and consecutive 12-month period determined by the employer, so your benefit year does not have to be the calendar year.

## What employees can use it for

Arizona's law allows paid sick time for a broader set of reasons than many employers expect, including:

- The employee's own illness, injury, health condition, medical care or preventive care
- Care of a family member for the same reasons
- Closure of the workplace or a child's school or place of care due to a public health emergency
- Absences related to domestic violence, sexual violence, abuse or stalking

Employers may require reasonable documentation in some situations, and there are limits on what can be asked. Retaliation for using sick time is prohibited.

## Where temps fit in

Here is where questions come up. When you bring in a worker through a staffing agency, **the agency is typically the employer of record** and handles accrual, tracking and payment of earned paid sick time for that worker. The hours a temp works on your site count toward their accrual with the agency, and when they use sick time, it comes through the agency's payroll.

A few points worth understanding:

### Accrual follows the worker, not the assignment

A temp who moves from one client to another with the same agency generally keeps building time with that agency. If that worker shows up on your floor already past their 90th day with the agency, they may be eligible to use accrued time from their first day with you.

### Absences still affect your schedule

Even though the agency pays the sick time, a protected absence still leaves a gap on your line. Ask your partner how they backfill when an assigned worker calls out for a covered reason. Sick time protections are one reason a deep bench matters more than a long roster.

### Employer size can get complicated

The 24-hour versus 40-hour cap depends on employee count. For staffing agencies, which can have large numbers of workers across many clients, that count is typically well above 15. If your own company is near the threshold, how temps factor into your count is a question for your employment counsel, not a guess.

### Temp-to-hire resets the relationship

When you convert a temp to your direct payroll, you become their employer for sick time purposes from that point. Talk with your HR advisor about how accrual and waiting periods apply to newly converted employees.

## Practical steps for host employers

1. **Know your partner's policy.** Ask how they track accrual, how workers request time, and how they notify you when a worker will be out.
2. **Build absence into staffing plans.** If you need 40 people on the floor, plan for coverage when a few use sick time, especially during flu season, which overlaps with peak.
3. **Keep supervisors out of the eligibility question.** Line leads should route absence questions to the agency and should never discourage a temp from using protected time.
4. **Review your own policy for direct employees.** Make sure your handbook, payroll system and pay stubs meet the law's notice and recordkeeping requirements.
5. **Check local rules.** Some cities have their own wage ordinances. If you operate outside the Phoenix metro, confirm whether additional rules apply.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Talk with your HR advisor or employment counsel about your specific situation.*

## The bottom line

Arizona's earned paid sick time law is not new, but it still trips up employers, particularly where temps and direct employees work side by side. The good news is that when you use a staffing agency, most of the administration sits with the agency. Your job is to plan for coverage and make sure supervisors handle absences correctly.
